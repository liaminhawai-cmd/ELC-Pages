What is an issue?

An issue is a topic people have different opinions about. It often affects people, communities or society in some way. If everyone agrees, it is not an issue.

🙋 Personal

Affects you and people your age directly.

🏘️ Local / Community

Affects your school, suburb or city.

🇦🇺 National

Affects the whole country.

social media age limitsschool funding

🌏 Global

Affects people everywhere.

climate changeAI and jobs

Fact or opinion?

Persuasive writers mix the two on purpose. Before you can judge an argument, you have to see which is which.

A fact can be checked and proven — it often has numbers, dates or a source. An opinion is what someone believes — look for judgement words like should, best, causes more harm than good.

The four things to find in any persuasive text

❗ Issue

The topic people disagree about. Usually named in the first lines.

🎯 Contention

The writer's main opinion. Look in the introduction or conclusion for strong opinion language: should, must, need to, should not.

Starter: "The writer believes that…"

🧱 Arguments & evidence

An argument is a reason for the contention. Evidence is what props the reason up — facts, statistics, examples, research.

🎭 Tone

The writer's attitude, in one word: positive, concerned, hopeful, serious, critical. You hear it in the word choices.

Your persuasive text

Pick one text type: a letter to the editor, an opinion article or a speech. Same skeleton for all three:

1️⃣ Introduction

Introduce the issue. State your contention clearly, and name your arguments.

2️⃣ Body paragraphs ×2–3

One argument per paragraph, with evidence, in TEEL: Topic sentence · Explain · Evidence · Link.

3️⃣ Conclusion

Restate your contention. End with a strong final message or call to action.

How a debate runs

Two teams of three. The affirmative agrees with the topic; the negative disagrees. Speakers alternate, and every speech follows the shape the rubric asks for: introduction → numbered arguments → rebuttals → conclusion.

Key vocabulary

Rebuttal sentence starters

A rebuttal answers a specific point the other team made — the rubric rewards naming their exact detail or example, not just disagreeing.

Name their point

"The first speaker for the affirmative argued that… "

Turn to it

"However, this argument ignores… "

Counter with evidence

"In fact, the evidence shows that… "

Close it off

"So this argument does not stand, because… "
