Learning intention

We are learning how a ballad uses story, rhyme, rhythm and voice, and how a writer can borrow that structure.

Success criteria

I can retell the events, identify AABBCC couplets, hear the beat, explain an unfamiliar word, and reshape a stanza while keeping its rhyme and line length.

Before we read

What is a ballad?

It tells a story

Characters act, a problem develops, and something changes.

It is made to be heard

A regular pulse, repeated patterns and memorable rhyme help a teller perform it.

It uses a speaking voice

Dialogue, exclamations and everyday language can make the action feel immediate.

It moves quickly

Ballads usually select vivid actions and leave the audience to infer some details.

Ballads do not all use one fixed pattern. Traditional ballads often use four-line stanzas. Paterson’s bush ballad uses six-line stanzas made from three rhyming couplets: AABBCC.
Set-up · 1–2A bush visitor enters a city barber shop.
Plan · 3–4The barber prepares a practical joke.
Reaction · 5–6The visitor believes it and fights back.
Aftermath · 7–8The joke is explained and retold at home.

Turn Ironbark into Kew

Editable workshop

Paterson’s first stanza works as the mentor text below. Make one small change first: swap Ironbark for Kew. That breaks the last rhyme pair, so the class has a real writing problem to solve — then pick any stanza and rewrite it the same way.

OriginalIt was the man from Ironbark …
→
First changeIt was the man from Kew …
1SwapChange only Ironbark to Kew.
2NoticeThe C pair no longer rhymes: mark / Kew.
3TogetherBrainstorm Kew rhymes and repair the stanza.
4Your versionKeep editing as much or as little as you choose.
