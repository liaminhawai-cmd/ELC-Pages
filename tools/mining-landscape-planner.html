Mining Landscape Planner

Choose where and when to mine. Read the map, interpret climate data, weigh cultural, aesthetic, economic and environmental values, then run the first season and explain what happened.

Your problem: The mine needs a place with economic value (minerals), but the landscape also has cultural value (Indigenous sacred places), economic value for farmers and graziers, environmental value in vegetation and waterways, and aesthetic value in scenic areas. If you mine in the wrong place — or in the wrong season — erosion, polluted runoff, conflict and loss of value can follow.

Cultural and spiritual value

The northern escarpment and waterholes hold cultural value for local Aboriginal communities. Two places are especially sensitive: Jindara Rock at B1 and the coolamon waterhole around A2. These are linked to Dreaming stories and are not suitable for mining or blasting.

Even where land is not fully off-limits, proximity matters. Noise, dust, traffic and landscape scarring can still reduce the spiritual value and aesthetic value of nearby places.

In this unit, cultural value refers to how landscapes and landforms are linked to the culture of a group. Spiritual value refers to places with deep belief, ceremony or identity significance.

Companies that avoid sacred places, consult properly and protect access routes are more likely to earn community trust.

Economic value and land use

This landscape already has economic value before any mining begins. Farms in the moderate-rainfall belt grow fodder crops and support local jobs. Grazing routes allow movement between seasonal paddocks and water points.

If mining blocks grazing corridors or damages farmland, there is a conflict between two different forms of economic value: mining profit versus agriculture and pastoral production.

Students should notice that a landscape can hold more than one kind of value at the same time. Economic value is not just mining profit.

Remote mineral-rich cells may look attractive, but haul roads, waste dumps and water demand still need to be considered.

Environmental and aesthetic value

Dense vegetation along rivers, wetlands and higher-rainfall areas provides habitat and helps stabilise soil. Removing it increases erosion, sediment in waterways and ecological damage.

Scenic areas, escarpments and waterholes also have aesthetic value. Even if a place is not sacred, people may value its beauty and the feeling of an intact landscape.

Aesthetic value is about beauty. Environmental value is about ecosystems, vegetation, habitat and land/water health.

A sustainable plan must reduce negative impacts on all these values, not just maximise short-term profit.
